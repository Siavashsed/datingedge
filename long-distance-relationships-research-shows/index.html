Long-Distance Relationships Survive Fine. The Reunion Is What Breaks Them.

Long-distance relationships aren't less stable than other couples. What the research shows about idealization, reunion risk, and attachment.

Photo: Vitaly Gariev
By Dr. Priya Sharma · November 26, 2026 · Dating & Relationships · 4 min read

Long-distance relationships don't fail more often than other relationships. What breaks many of them is the day the distance ends. I watched this happen in 2019 with a couple I'll call Mara and Tomas, who spent 14 months split between Chicago and Lisbon, talked every morning at 7 a.m. Chicago time, and fell apart eleven weeks after he moved into her one-bedroom apartment. The calls had been the best conversations either of them had ever had. The arguments over who stacked the dishwasher wrong were not.

I've worked with attachment and modern couples for years, and the same pattern keeps showing up. People ask me whether distance will kill their relationship. Wrong question. The research points somewhere less comfortable, and I've written about the broader evidence in what the research actually shows about long-distance relationships. Here's the sharper version.

Distance Itself Isn't the Problem the Data Points To

When researchers compare couples who live apart with couples who live close, the gap in satisfaction mostly vanishes. A widely cited study by Kelmer and colleagues, published in 2013 in Family Process, found that long-distance partners reported relationship quality, commitment, and stability on par with geographically close couples. Not worse. Roughly the same.

There's an odder finding on top of that. Jiang and Hancock's 2013 work in the Journal of Communication showed that long-distance partners disclosed more to each other and idealized each other more, and reported feeling more intimate as a result. Distance made them talk like people with something to prove.

So why does everyone still believe these relationships are doomed? Because we remember the dramatic endings, and because a half-finished version of the story gets told at dinner parties. The failure rate isn't the scandal. The timing of the failures is.

Why the Reunion Is the Danger Zone

Idealization works until it meets a toothbrush

Idealization is the engine behind that extra intimacy, and it's also the trap. Stafford and Merolla's 2007 study in the Journal of Social and Personal Relationships found that about a third of long-distance couples who finally moved to the same city broke up within three months. The couples who had idealized each other most were the ones hit hardest by the correction.

You've been dating a curated person. Forty-five minutes of full attention on a video call isn't the same as watching someone be tired, short with a waiter, and unable to find their keys. And here's the thing... they've been dating a curated version of you as well.

Loss of autonomy is the quiet killer

Couples apart for a year or more build entire routines on their own. Moving in together means someone's gym schedule, sleep schedule, or friend group gets rewritten. In my practice I've seen this cost people more than any fight does. Nobody warns you that getting what you wanted can feel like a small bereavement.

What Distance Does to Attachment Styles

This is where my clinical lens matters more than the headline numbers. Distance doesn't treat attachment styles equally. If you lean anxious, silence between calls becomes a fire alarm. A delayed text can feel like abandonment, and you start protesting in ways that look like control. If you lean avoidant, distance is oddly comfortable. You get intimacy on a schedule you control, which is partly why avoidant partners sometimes thrive apart and stall the moment the relationship asks for daily presence.

In my work with long-distance couples, the anxious-avoidant pairing is the most common one I see, and it's the one where distance quietly masks the mismatch. Apart, the anxious partner gets scheduled reassurance and the avoidant partner gets space. Same city, and both needs collide at 6 p.m. on a Tuesday.

I'm not 100% sure this part generalizes, so take it with salt. But my own situation backs it up a little: in my late twenties I dated someone in Toronto for about nine months, and I once drove to the airport to surprise him because he'd gone quiet for a day and a half. He was asleep. With the flu. I sat in arrivals eating a $9 pretzel and rehearsing a speech about trust that I'm still embarrassed by. So yes, I know what an anxious spiral looks like from the inside, and it isn't pretty. What I Tell Couples to Actually Do

Don't treat the distance as a temporary tunnel you grit through. That framing is the biggest mistake I see. Couples who survive tend to have a concrete end date or a decision date, not a vague someday. Without one, the relationship drifts in a permanent waiting room, and the waiting itself becomes the relationship.

Quality of contact matters more than quantity. Constant texting often feeds anxiety rather than easing it. If your calls have gone stale, try a better prompt than 'how was your day', and the conversation topics that create real connection work just as well on a video call as at a restaurant table.

Before anyone books a one-way ticket, I ask couples to do a few specific things:

  • Spend at least two full weeks together in ordinary life, not on holiday, with work and chores included.
  • Talk through who gives up what, in writing, before the move.
  • Agree on a check-in at the three-month mark, since that's when the research shows trouble surfaces.

One more honest caveat. Plenty of couples meet through apps now and go long distance by accident of geography. As I noted when looking at Hinge's 2022 push to be designed to be deleted, apps are shifting expectations toward serious relationships faster. That can pull people into long-distance commitments before they've seen each other's bad days. It doesn't always end badly. It does mean the reunion test should come early, not late.

Tonight, put a date on the calendar for a two-week stretch of ordinary life together, and treat it as an audition, not a vacation. Do that before you sign a lease or quit a job. If you can stand each other's dishwasher habits for fourteen days, the research says your odds are good.

Frequently asked questions

Do long-distance relationships actually work?

Yes, in most cases. Research from 2013 found long-distance couples report satisfaction, commitment, and stability similar to couples who live near each other. The riskiest stretch is often the first few months after the couple finally lives in the same place.

Why do long-distance couples break up after moving in together?

Idealization built up during the distance runs into daily reality. A 2007 study found about a third of couples split within three months of reuniting, often over lost autonomy and clashing routines they'd never seen on a call.

How long can a long-distance relationship last without a plan?

There's no fixed number, but couples without a concrete end date tend to drift. Agree on a decision date, even a rough one, so the distance has a purpose rather than becoming the permanent shape of the relationship.
