Why a Situationship Wrecks You More Than a Clean Breakup

Situationships hurt more than breakups because your brain can't grieve what was never defined. A psychologist on why the ambiguity is the injury.

Photo: hitesh choudhary
By Pippa Sennett · July 15, 2026 · Dating & Relationships · 3 min read

An undefined relationship hurts more than a breakup because your brain can't close a door that was never fully opened. There's no ending to mourn, so you loop instead of heal. A client I saw last spring put it better than any textbook I own. She said, "At least when someone dumps you, you get to be sad. I don't even know if I'm allowed to be sad." She'd been seeing a guy for eight months, texting daily, meeting his friends, and he still called her "my person" instead of anything with a shape. That vagueness is the whole problem.

I've spent years working with people caught in these half-relationships, and the pattern is almost always the same. It's not the other person's coldness that breaks them. It's the not-knowing. The constant low hum of trying to read tone in a text, of deciding whether "good morning" means what you hope it means. Uncertainty is its own kind of pain, and we're wired to feel it acutely.

Your nervous system hates ambiguity more than bad news

Here's the part most breakup advice misses. Certainty, even painful certainty, lets your body stand down. Ambiguity keeps the alarm on. A 2016 study published in Nature Communications found that people felt more stress when there was a 50 percent chance of getting a mild electric shock than when they knew for sure a shock was coming. Read that again. Not-knowing spiked cortisol higher than knowing something bad was guaranteed.

That's the physiology of a situationship. Every unanswered "where is this going" leaves you in that 50-50 zone. Your body treats it like a threat that never resolves, so you stay braced. In my work with clients, this shows up as sleep that won't come, checking a phone forty times an hour, appetite that vanishes. The undefined bond keeps your stress system switched on in a way a clean ending simply doesn't.

A breakup is a shock. A situationship is a slow leak. And the leak, arguably, does more damage over time.

You can't grieve something that was never named

Psychologist Pauline Boss coined a phrase I use with clients constantly. She called it ambiguous loss, the grief that has no closure because the thing you lost was never clearly there or clearly gone. It was first studied in families of soldiers missing in action, people who couldn't mourn and couldn't hope. The parallel to modern dating is uncomfortable but real.

When a defined relationship ends, you get a set of rituals. You tell your friends. You delete the photos, or you don't. You get to say "my ex." Those words are small, but they mark the loss as real and give your mind permission to process it. A situationship denies you all of that. You lost someone you were never officially with, so even your grief feels like it needs an apology.

So people minimize. They tell me, "It wasn't even a real thing, I shouldn't be this upset." But the attachment was real even if the label wasn't. Your heart doesn't check for a title before it bonds.

The intermittent reward is what keeps you stuck

Let me be honest about something, because I think the advice industry pretends this is simpler than it is. My closest friend spent most of last year in one of these, and she has a doctorate in this exact field, and even she couldn't walk away for months. She'd tell me over wine that she knew, clinically, that she was hooked on the unpredictability, and then she'd check her phone mid-sentence to see if he'd replied. We laughed about it. It wasn't really funny. I'm not 100 percent sure any of us are immune, is what I'm saying, no matter how many books we've read.

The mechanism underneath is intermittent reinforcement. When affection arrives unpredictably, sometimes warm, sometimes gone for three days, your brain's reward system fires harder than it would for steady, reliable care. It's the same loop that makes slot machines sticky. The inconsistency isn't a bug you can fix by trying harder. It's the hook itself.

This is also why leaving feels physically difficult. You're not weak. You're responding to a reward schedule engineered, unintentionally usually, to keep you guessing.

How to tell a slow burn from a dead end

Not every undefined relationship is a trap. Some people genuinely need time, and rushing a label can wreck something good. So the honest question isn't "are we official yet." It's whether the ambiguity is moving toward clarity or just sitting there.

Here's what I watch for with clients trying to make the call:

  • Direction. After a real conversation about the future, does anything actually change, or do you get warmth in the moment and the same fog by next week?
  • Consistency. Reliable people are boring in the best way. If the pattern is hot-then-silent on a loop, that's data, not a phase.
  • Your own state. Around this person, are you calmer or more anxious over time? Real closeness lowers your baseline. A situationship raises it.

The Match Singles in America research has tracked situationships as a rising category among younger daters for a few years now, which tells me this isn't a personal failing. It's a cultural default. That doesn't mean you have to accept it. Distance and ambiguity feed on each other, something I got into more in what the research actually shows about long-distance relationships, and the same clarity test applies there too.

Do one thing today. Ask the direct question once, in plain words, out loud or by text: "I like you, and I want to know if we're heading toward being together or not." Then watch what happens in the next seven days, not the next seven minutes. A real answer changes behavior. A situationship changes only the temperature of the room while the fog stays exactly where it was. If nothing shifts, you finally have the certainty your nervous system has been begging for, and you can let yourself grieve it like the real loss it always was.

Frequently asked questions

Why does a situationship hurt more than an actual breakup?

Because there's no clear ending to grieve, your brain stays stuck in unresolved uncertainty, which research links to higher stress than a known bad outcome. You also feel you have no permission to mourn something that was never officially defined, so the pain lingers instead of closing.

How long should I wait for someone to define a situationship?

Watch behavior over roughly one to two months after you clearly ask, not just words in the moment. If a direct conversation about the future changes nothing and the hot-then-cold pattern repeats, that's your answer. Reliable people show effort, not just occasional warmth.

Am I weak for not being able to leave a situationship?

No. Unpredictable affection triggers intermittent reinforcement, the same reward loop that makes gambling addictive, so your brain gets hooked on the inconsistency itself. Struggling to leave is a normal response to that pattern, not a character flaw.
