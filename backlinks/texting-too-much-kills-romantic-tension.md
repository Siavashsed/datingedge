**Title:** Does anyone else feel the spark fade when you text constantly in the first few weeks?

I'm a psychologist, and something I keep seeing in sessions is that constant texting early on tends to flatten attraction rather than build it.

Attraction runs largely on not knowing. If someone has heard about your lunch, your commute and your mood swings by 3 p.m., there's very little left to be curious about when you finally sit down across from each other at 8.

One client showed me her phone: over 400 messages with a man she'd met on Hinge nine days earlier. It felt like intimacy, but it was really a stream of low-stakes updates. By the first date they were oddly flat with each other, like a couple who'd already run out of things to say.

A few things that seem to help:

- Let a conversation end naturally instead of filling every gap.
- Save stories and bigger moments for in person.
- Notice whether you're texting from interest or from anxiety. Those feel similar but lead to very different places.
- Replying quickly isn't the same as connecting deeply.

I'm curious whether others have noticed the same thing, or whether heavy texting worked for you.

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** Can texting too much kill romantic tension and attraction early in a relationship?

Yes, and it's something I see regularly in my clinical practice as a psychologist specializing in attachment and modern relationships.

Attraction runs on a gap between what you know and what you'd like to know. That gap is curiosity, and curiosity is what makes someone feel magnetic. Constant texting closes the gap quickly. If a person has heard about your lunch, your commute and your mood swings by mid-afternoon, there is very little left to wonder about when you meet that evening.

There's a second problem. Texting feels like intimacy, but it is mostly a stream of low-stakes updates. Real closeness comes from being seen in context: tone of voice, body language, how someone handles a pause. A few hundred messages can create the sensation of knowing someone without any of that information. People then arrive at the first date emotionally attached to a version of the person they've assembled from text, and the real person often feels flat or different by comparison.

The third factor is anxiety. Many people text constantly not because they're interested but because waiting for a reply is uncomfortable, and messaging soothes that discomfort. That pattern can quietly shape the relationship around reassurance instead of attraction.

What tends to help:

1. Let conversations end naturally rather than filling every silence.
2. Save stories, jokes and bigger topics for in-person time.
3. Ask yourself whether you're texting from interest or from nervousness.
4. Move to a call or a date sooner rather than building a long text history.
