Here's the framing that finally made it click for me: attention and effort are not the same currency. Someone who's bored gives you attention. It's cheap, instant, and costs them nothing. - Volume of messages with no movement toward meeting
- Contact clustered late at night or in dead time
- Vague questions instead of specific ones
- Warmth that disappears the moment you ask for something concrete

None of this is a rule, and people can be anxious or shy without being disinterested. But if the texting never converts into a plan after a couple of weeks, that's usually your answer.

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** How can you tell if someone is genuinely interested in you or just bored and texting you for attention?

As a clinical psychologist who works with attachment and modern relationships, the distinction I give clients is this: a bored person gives you attention, while an interested person gives you effort. They are not the same currency.

Attention is cheap. Sending a meme, a "what are you up to," or a late-night "hey" costs almost nothing and gives the sender a small hit of stimulation. Effort costs something. It takes time, planning, and a willingness to risk a no.

I once worked with a client I'll call Dana, who showed me 212 messages from one man over eleven days. Nearly all were late at night, nearly all low-stakes. He never once suggested meeting. She had spent two weeks analyzing every message, when the pattern itself was the answer.

Here is what I look for:

1. Specificity. Interested people name a day, a place, a plan. Bored people stay vague.
2. Memory. They reference something you told them earlier, which shows they were actually listening.
3. Consistency in daylight. If contact only appears late at night or in dead time, you may be a time-filler.
4. Follow-through. Warmth that vanishes when you ask for something concrete is a strong signal.
5. Movement. Real interest tends to progress, from text to call to meeting, instead of looping in place.

Two cautions. First, anxious or shy people can be slow to make plans without being uninterested, so look at the whole pattern, not one message. Second, if you find yourself decoding someone for weeks, that in itself is information. Clarity rarely requires that much analysis.

A simple test: suggest something specific and low-pressure, such as coffee on Thursday. Their response will tell you more than another hundred messages.
