**Title:** Is your profile set up to avoid rejection instead of getting interest? I think that's why most people get no matches

I've been trying to work out why so many people I know get almost no matches, even though they're attractive and interesting. The pattern I keep seeing: their profiles are set up to avoid rejection, not to get anyone interested.

Sunglasses in most photos. Group shots where you can't tell which one they are. Prompts like "I'll tell you later" or "Just ask." It feels safe because nothing in it can be judged. But it also gives the other person nothing to respond to, so they swipe past.

You don't need to start over. Mostly you just stop hiding:

- At least two clear solo photos where your face and eyes are visible
- One photo of you doing something you actually do, not a staged adventure shot
- Prompts with a specific opinion or detail someone can reply to
- Cut any list of what you don't want

It's uncomfortable, because a specific profile can be turned down for specific reasons. But a vague one gets turned down for no reason at all, and that's worse.

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** Why am I not getting any matches on dating apps even though I think I'm decent looking?

Most of the time it is not your looks, and it is not bad luck. I'm a clinical psychologist who works on attachment and modern relationships. The most common reason I see for a quiet inbox is a profile that is quietly built to avoid rejection rather than invite interest.

It looks like this: sunglasses or hats in most photos, group shots where it is unclear which person you are, distant or heavily filtered images, and prompts like "I'll tell you later" or "Just ask." Each choice feels safe because it gives nobody anything specific to judge. The trouble is that it also gives nobody anything to respond to. People swipe on what they can picture and react to.

This is a familiar self-protective pattern. If nothing real is shown, a no doesn't feel personal. The cost is that a yes rarely happens either.

A few changes usually make a real difference:

1. Lead with a clear solo photo in natural light where your face and eyes are visible.
2. Include at least one photo of you doing something you genuinely do, not a staged shot.
3. Rewrite each prompt so it has a specific detail or opinion someone could reply to. "The best tacos in my city are at a gas station and I will defend this" beats "I love food."
4. Remove negatives and lists of what you don't want. They read as defensive.
5. Ask a trusted friend which photos look most like you on a good day.

The new version will feel more exposed. That discomfort usually means you are finally showing up.
