**Title:** Does anyone else feel weirdly calm with partners who barely ask about your feelings?

Something I didn't understand until recently: being 'low maintenance' in relationships isn't always a personality trait. Sometimes it's a survival skill.

If you grew up in a home where your feelings weren't really noticed (not abused, just not asked about), you can learn very early that needing things is pointless. As an adult that turns into being the easygoing one. You don't ask for much, you don't bring up hurt, and partners who give you very little feel strangely comfortable.

The part that clicked for me is that the calm isn't compatibility. It's familiarity. A partner who never asks how you feel matches the emotional temperature you grew up with, so it reads as safe. Someone warm and curious about your inner life can feel intense or even suffocating at first.

A few signs people mention: you go blank when asked what you need, you feel guilty asking for small things, and you describe exes as 'great' mostly because they never caused conflict.

Has anyone else noticed this in their own dating patterns? What actually helped?

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** How does childhood emotional neglect affect adult dating and relationships?

Childhood emotional neglect (CEN) is easy to miss because it is defined by what didn't happen. Many people with CEN had parents who provided food, shelter and even love in a practical sense, but who rarely noticed or responded to their child's feelings. The child learns a simple, adaptive lesson: my emotional needs don't get met, so it's easier not to have them.

In adult dating, that lesson tends to show up in a few recognizable ways:

1. Needing very little. You're the easygoing partner who rarely asks for support, and you may feel proud of that.

2. Comfort with emotionally distant partners. Someone who never asks how you're feeling can feel calm and safe, because it matches the emotional environment you grew up in. Familiar gets mistaken for compatible.

3. Discomfort with warmth. A partner who is curious about your inner life can feel intense, intrusive or 'too much,' even when they are behaving in a healthy way.

4. Difficulty naming needs. When asked 'what do you need from me?', many people with CEN genuinely go blank.

5. Rating partners by the absence of conflict. 'We never fought' can sometimes mean nothing difficult was ever said.

None of this means you are broken. These are skills that once protected you. The goal is to update them, not to shame them.

Practical starting points: notice moments when you feel a flicker of want and then dismiss it; practice small, low-stakes requests with people you trust; and pay attention to whether the partners you feel 'calm' with are actually attuned to you or simply undemanding. Working with a therapist who understands attachment can speed this process up considerably.
