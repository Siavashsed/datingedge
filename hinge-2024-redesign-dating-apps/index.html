Hinge's 2024 'It's Designed to Be Deleted' Rebrand Was a Turning Point Nobody Saw Coming

Dr. Priya Sharma on Hinge's 2024 redesign and what it revealed about where dating apps-and the people using them-actually stand.

Photo: Werner Pfennig
By Dr. Priya Sharma · October 20, 2024 · Dating & Relationships · 5 min read

Hinge made a genuinely bold move in 2024, and most people are still underestimating what it means. In early 2024, Hinge rolled out its most significant product overhaul since launch, doubling down on its 'Designed to Be Deleted' positioning with a redesigned interface built explicitly around getting users off the app faster and into actual relationships. I sat with a client in late January who had been on Hinge for almost two years. She pulled out her phone mid-session to show me the new profile layout, and her exact words were, 'This finally feels like it's on my side.' That stuck with me. Because she wasn't commenting on the design. She was commenting on intent.

The redesign wasn't just cosmetic. Hinge restructured its prompts, pushed compatibility features harder, and made 'Your Turn' notifications more aggressive about nudging users to close the loop on conversations that were going nowhere. The underlying message was pointed: stop scrolling, start committing. For an industry that has historically profited from keeping you hooked, that's a significant public posture to take. Whether Hinge fully lives up to it is a separate question, and I'll get to that. But the direction it signals matters for everyone using apps right now.

What Hinge Actually Changed and Why It Was Different This Time

The 2024 redesign introduced several structural changes that are worth separating from the marketing spin. Hinge expanded its 'Prompt' categories to include more values-based and lifestyle questions, reduced the visual weight of photos relative to written answers, and introduced 'Most Compatible' suggestions driven by its Nobel Prize-winning economist-advised algorithm. That last part sounds like press release language, and honestly, it partly is. But the principle behind it is grounded: research published in PNAS in 2022 found that similarity in values and communication style predicts relationship satisfaction more reliably than physical attractiveness ratings in early app interactions. Hinge's algorithm is trying to operationalize exactly that.

The notification overhaul was the more quietly significant change. Hinge began flagging conversations that had stalled for more than 72 hours and prompting both users to either re-engage or unmatch. In my work with clients who struggle with anxious attachment, this kind of forced resolution is actually useful. The ambiguity of a lingering match that never goes anywhere is one of the most underrated sources of low-grade anxiety I see in people who date heavily through apps. Hinge is, at least in structure, trying to cut that off.

And the visual redesign moved the app away from the endless scroll format that competitors like Tinder built their early dominance on. If you're curious how much that scroll mechanic shaped modern app behavior, the story of Tinder's 2019 Swipe Night launch is a good place to see how deliberately addictive design choices became normalized across the industry. Hinge is consciously walking back from that model. That's not nothing. For more on this, see Hinge's 2022 'Designed to Be Deleted' Push: What It Actually Meant for How We Date.

The Market Context Behind the Timing of This Shift

Hinge didn't make these changes in a vacuum. The broader dating app market had a rough 2023 and entered 2024 under real pressure. Match Group, which owns Hinge, reported that paying users across its portfolio dropped by around 5% year-over-year in late 2023, according to Match Group's investor relations filings. That's not catastrophic, but it's a signal. People are spending more on subscriptions and feeling like they're getting less out of them. Burnout is real, and it's showing up in the data.

Bumble has been dealing with a version of this same problem since its post-IPO period. If you want context on how platform economics and user trust get misaligned, the Bumble 2022 IPO hangover story laid out exactly how that breaks down. Hinge watched that and made a calculation: in a contracting market, the brand that users actually trust is the one that survives. So the 2024 redesign is partly a product decision and partly a survival strategy.

Pew Research data from 2023 found that 45% of Americans who had used a dating app described the experience as 'frustrating.' That number has been climbing. Hinge's bet is that if it can genuinely reduce that frustration and get people to actual dates faster, they'll attract the users who've quit and rebuild trust with the ones who stayed. It's a longer-term play than most app companies have been willing to make.

What This Means Psychologically for People Using Apps Right Now

Here's where I want to push back on the optimism a little. The redesign is genuinely better. But there's a persistent misconception in how people interpret 'better design' on a dating app, and I see it constantly in my practice. Better design doesn't fix avoidant attachment patterns. It doesn't make someone who's emotionally unavailable suddenly available. It doesn't resolve the approach-avoidance conflict that keeps people cycling through apps without ever committing to a real conversation. If your profile isn't generating meaningful matches, the problem is almost never the platform, and a new interface won't change that. There's a useful breakdown of what actually drives match quality at the profile level in this piece on why your dating app profile isn't getting matches, and it's worth reading alongside any platform update. For more on this, see Hinge's 'Most Compatible' Overhaul in 2023 Changed How We Think About Algorithmic Dating.

What the redesign can do, and this matters, is reduce friction for people who are already willing to connect. I'm not 100% sure this works for everyone, but in my experience, a lot of the app fatigue I see isn't about emotional unavailability. It's structural. People get overwhelmed by choice, let conversations die out of inertia, and then feel bad about themselves for being 'bad at apps.' My friend Neha tried the updated Hinge in February and texted me, 'Why is it now telling me I have five conversations I abandoned, I feel like the app is my therapist.' Which, honestly? That's the feature working. Mild social accountability nudges can interrupt the passive scrolling habit. I've seen this pattern in clinical contexts with habit formation, and it transfers here.

The bigger psychological shift is about intention signaling. When a platform explicitly tells you it wants you to leave it, the users who remain there are, at least in theory, self-selecting for readiness. That changes the social contract of the app. It's subtle. But it affects behavior. And for people who have anxious attachment tendencies and tend to over-invest in matches that are clearly not reciprocated, an environment that pushes for resolution faster is genuinely protective.

Whether Hinge Can Actually Deliver on the Promise It's Making

The honest answer is: partially. Hinge's internal data, cited in its own marketing, claims users are going on more dates per month than on any other app. That's hard to independently verify, and I take it with appropriate skepticism. But the directional claim is plausible. The structural changes do reduce the distance between matching and meeting. The prompts do create more surface area for genuine compatibility signaling. And the reduced visual emphasis on photos does, at least marginally, shift selection behavior toward personality-based criteria.

What Hinge can't control is what happens after the app is deleted. The real test of whether this redesign matters isn't user satisfaction scores in 2024. It's whether the relationships that form through these updated interactions are more stable at 12 months than the ones formed before. We don't have that data yet. And until we do, treating this as a solved problem would be premature. For more on this, see Bumble's 2023 Rebrand Was a Turning Point Nobody Talked About Enough.

What I'd argue is that Hinge has done something genuinely rare in this industry: it has publicly committed to a metric that conflicts with engagement. Most apps optimize for time on platform. Hinge is publicly saying it optimizes for time off platform. That tension will either produce something meaningfully different, or it will get quietly abandoned when Match Group needs to improve quarterly numbers. Watching which way that goes over the next 18 months is, in my view, the most important story in the dating app space right now.

If you're using Hinge right now, the one concrete thing to do is treat the new prompts seriously. Not as a performance, but as a filter. Write answers that would genuinely repel the wrong person. The 2024 redesign gave you better tools for self-disclosure, and the research is clear that honest, specific self-presentation generates fewer but higher-quality matches. Use the interface the way it was intended and let it do its job.
